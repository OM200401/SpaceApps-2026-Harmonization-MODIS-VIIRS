Method · NASA Space Apps 2026 · Harmonization of MODIS and VIIRS

Two satellites, one fire record

From 2000 to 2011 only MODIS watched fires from orbit. From 2012 VIIRS on S-NPP watches too, and it counts three to six times more fire pixels. Joined naively, fire seems to explode in 2012. To get one consistent record from 2000 to today, we estimate what S-NPP would have counted in the MODIS years. This page shows how, and how well it works.

\[ \hat S \;=\; \underbrace{M}_{\text{MODIS count}} \times \underbrace{r_{\text{block}}}_{\text{local ratio}} \times \underbrace{e^{\,f(x)}}_{\text{learned correction}} \]

Step 1 · The problem

Two cameras that count fires differently

A satellite fire product does not count fires. It counts pixels that look hot. MODIS pixels are about 1 km across. VIIRS pixels on S-NPP are 375 m, so about seven fit inside one MODIS pixel. A small fire fills a large share of a VIIRS pixel and stands out. The same fire is a speck inside a MODIS pixel and is often missed.

Same fire, two grids, drawn to scale. Big hot fires show up in both. Small, cool or smouldering fires show up mostly in VIIRS. So the ratio S/M depends on what kind of fire it is, which is why one fixed ratio is not enough.

The sensors also pass at different times. MODIS flies on Terra (about 10:30 am and pm) and Aqua (about 1:30 pm and am). S-NPP crosses at about 1:30, close to Aqua. Fires grow through the afternoon, so Terra and Aqua see different amounts, and the model treats them separately.

The model learns the MODIS → S-NPP link where both sensors overlap (blue box), then applies it backwards to the MODIS-only years. After 2021 Terra and Aqua drift off their usual overpass times (hatched), so those years don't look like 2000–2011 and are left out of the fit.

Step 2 · Before any math

Clean the data so both sensors count the same thing

About half of the gain over our first rule came from going back to the raw detections and fixing four problems. A model can't learn a clean relationship from dirty pairs.

Orbit drift after 2021

Terra's average overpass slid from 10:33 to 09:26, and Aqua's from 13:36 to 14:45, by 2025. Morning and late-afternoon fire differ, so S/M shifts. We fit only on June 2012 – 2021, when overpass times were flat.

Outage days

Aqua was out 16 Aug – 2 Sep 2020; S-NPP lost 16 days in 2022 and 25 in 2024. When any sensor is out, that day is dropped for all of them, so each pair compares the same days. In the MODIS years a month is scaled back up by days in month ÷ days kept.

Gas flares

Oil-field flares burn all year, mostly at night, where S-NPP is far more sensitive than MODIS. They are industry, not wildfire. A 0.02° cell is masked when:

\[\begin{cases}\text{it burns in} \ge 8 \text{ of 12 calendar months}\\ \text{in} \ge 15 \text{ separate months (2012–2021)}\\ \ge 50\%\text{ of its detections at night}\end{cases}\]

Plus 0.1° cells burning in ≥ 30 months, ≥ 10 calendar months, ≥ 75% at night (wells move inside an oil field). Removes about 1.7% of S-NPP detections. Crop and savanna burning is seasonal and daytime, so it passes.

A slow start

S-NPP read about 20% low in March 2012 compared with its 2013–2019 average. Training starts in June 2012.

Step 3 · The baseline

A local ratio that trusts data only as far as it goes

The simplest conversion is a ratio: over all training months, how many S-NPP detections per MODIS detection?

\[ r = \frac{\sum S}{\sum M} \]

One ratio for the whole world is too blunt: Canadian crown fires and African savanna fires behave differently. A ratio per 1° block is local, but many blocks have only a handful of MODIS detections, and a ratio from five detections is mostly noise. So each level borrows strength from the level above (shrinkage):

\[ r_{\text{block}} = \frac{\sum S_{\text{block}} + k\, r_{\text{tile}}}{\sum M_{\text{block}} + k} \;=\; w\cdot\frac{\sum S_{\text{block}}}{\sum M_{\text{block}}} + (1-w)\, r_{\text{tile}}, \qquad w = \frac{\sum M_{\text{block}}}{\sum M_{\text{block}} + k} \]

In words: pretend the block has k extra MODIS detections that behave like its 10° tile. Lots of history: the block's own ratio wins. Little history: the tile's ratio wins.

With k = 200 for blocks, a block with 200 MODIS detections of history is trusted half-way; one with 2,000 about 91%. Tiles shrink toward the world ratio the same way with k = 2,000. Every ratio is computed separately for day and night.

Ratios are also built per block × month of the year and per tile × month of the year. They are not multiplied in: they go to the model in Step 4 as inputs, so it can decide how much seasonality matters.

Step 4 · The correction

Gradient boosting learns when the ratio is wrong

The local ratio is a long-run average. In a given month it can be off: a month of intense crown fires gives fewer S-NPP detections per MODIS detection than a month of small smouldering fires in the same block. So the baseline is multiplied by a correction learned from that month's MODIS detections:

\[ \hat S = M\, r_{\text{block}} \cdot e^{f(x)} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln \hat S = \ln(M\, r_{\text{block}}) + f(x) \]

In words: \(f = 0\) means "the ratio is right". \(f = 0.2\) means "about 22% more than the ratio says". \(f = -0.2\), about 18% less.

What goes into x

Only things MODIS can tell us, because in 2000–2011 MODIS is all there is. Every input comes from that block-month's MODIS detections or its neighbours.

Boosting: many small trees added together

\(f(x)\) is not one formula. It is the sum of 400 small decision trees (LightGBM), each fixing what the previous ones got wrong:

\[ f(x) = \eta\,T_1(x) + \eta\,T_2(x) + \dots + \eta\,T_{400}(x), \qquad \eta = 0.05 \]

Each tree asks a few yes/no questions about x and ends in a leaf. Here the leaves are linear: a leaf returns a small straight-line formula of the inputs, so the correction changes smoothly inside a leaf. The small learning rate \(\eta\) means each tree moves the answer only 5% of the way.

Poisson loss: the right yardstick for counts

To grow each tree the model scores how wrong it is. Detections are counts, and count noise grows with the count. Squared error would let a few giant fire months dominate. We use the Poisson loss:

\[ \ell(S, \hat S) = \hat S - S \ln \hat S, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \ln \hat S} = \hat S - S, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial (\ln \hat S)^2} = \hat S \]

In words: each tree pushes toward the miss \(S - \hat S\), scaled by \(1/\hat S\). Missing by 50 is a big error where 100 are expected and a small one where 10,000 are. That is relative error, which is what matters.

No peeking. The block ratios fed in as inputs are computed without the row's own year. Otherwise a row's ratio would already contain its answer, the model would over-trust the ratio, and that trust would fail in 2005, where there is no S-NPP to build the ratio from.

Step 5 · Edge cases

Three cases, depending on what MODIS saw

The main formula multiplies by M. If MODIS saw nothing in that block and half of the day, M = 0 and the formula says zero. But S-NPP often does see something there: exactly the small fires MODIS misses. So block-months are split three ways.

MODIS saw fire this half of the day

Main model

\[\hat S = M\, r_{\text{block}}\, e^{f(x)}\]
Almost all detections. Steps 3 and 4.
No, but MODIS saw fire at the other time of day, or only low-confidence pixels

Zero model

\[\hat S = (M_{o}\, r_x + c_0)\, e^{g(x)}\]
\(M_o\) = MODIS in the other half of the day; \(r_x\) = S-NPP per other-half MODIS detection, shrunk the same way (k = 50 block, 500 tile); \(c_0\) = average S-NPP when MODIS has only low-confidence pixels; \(g\) = a smaller boosted model. About 4% of night-time S-NPP.
No MODIS evidence at all

Climatology

\[\hat S = \frac{\sum_{\text{no-evidence years}} S}{\#\text{ no-evidence years}}\]
The block's average S-NPP in that month and half of the day, over training years when MODIS also saw nothing.

Before July 2002: only Terra

Aqua launched in 2002, so from November 2000 to June 2002 MODIS means Terra alone, three hours before S-NPP. A second copy of the whole model is fitted on 2012–2021 with Aqua removed and used for those months. That also gives a fair test: hide Aqua in the overlap years and see how well Terra alone predicts S-NPP (world error 2.7%, against 6.4% for our first Terra ratio).

 Step 6 · To the map
 
From 1° blocks to 0.25° cells, and joining at 2012

 

  
The app draws 0.25° cells (about 25 km), 16 per block. Each block's estimate is split by where MODIS saw the fire:

  
\[ \hat S_{\text{cell}} = \hat S_{\text{block}} \cdot \frac{M_{\text{cell}}}{M_{\text{block}}} \]

  
When MODIS saw nothing in the block (zero model and climatology), the split uses each cell's share of the block's S-NPP over 2012–2021. Fill values under 0.5 in cells with no MODIS are dropped to keep the map files small (0.24% of the total).

  
From February 2012 nothing is estimated. The record is what S-NPP saw, minus gas flares, so both eras count the same thing:

  
\[ H = \begin{cases} \hat S \text{ converted from MODIS} & \text{Nov 2000 – Jan 2012} \\ S_{\text{observed}} - S_{\text{flares}} & \text{Feb 2012 onward} \end{cases} \]

  
The test of the join is the seam: if the conversion is right, there is no artificial jump at 2012. Worldwide, 2011 → 2012 rises 5.6% (our first rule: 6.8%). For Canada the harmonized change tracks MODIS alone within about one point, as the chart in Step 1 shows. 2012 really was a bigger fire year there.

 







 Step 7 · Did it work?
 
Testing on years the model never saw

 
Leave one year out. Fit on nine of the ten years 2012–2021, predict the tenth, repeat ten times. Every prediction is for a year the model did not train on, the same situation as 2005.

 

  
Each row is one fit. Blue years train the model; the orange year is predicted and scored.

 

  
The score is WAPE, weighted absolute percentage error, on monthly totals per region (day + night, all blocks in the region):

  
\[ \text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{\text{months}} |\hat S - S|}{\sum_{\text{months}} S} \]

  
In words: what share of the true detections we got wrong. Unlike an average of monthly percentages, a tiny winter month can't blow it up.

  
The floor. How good could any rule be? NOAA-20 carries the same VIIRS instrument as S-NPP and passes 50 minutes apart. Predicting S-NPP from NOAA-20 with block ratios gives the error between two identical sensors. No MODIS rule can beat that.

Other checks

  
Forward in time. Fit 2012–2018, predict 2019–2021: Canada 6.7%, Amazon 2.7%, Mediterranean 6.9%, US West 6.1%, Siberia 4.2%, world 1.7%.

  
Seeds. Averaging three random seeds changes nothing beyond 0.1 points.

  
Flare mask leak. The mask uses all of 2012–2021, including the held-out year. Rebuilding it without each held-out year changes 0.2–0.4% of that year's S-NPP detections, which bounds the effect at about half a point.

  
Where it still misses

  
Canada (6.5%): megafire months. Six months carry about 60% of the error, like the Northwest Territories in July 2014 (−24%) and British Columbia in August 2018 (−11%). There S/M jumps from about 5 to 10–29 per block, because smoke and smouldering ground hide fire from MODIS.

  
Mediterranean (7.3%): fires MODIS never sees. 8% of S-NPP detections fall in block-months with no same-time MODIS detection. No MODIS input can describe a fire MODIS didn't register.

  
The floor shows these months are predictable with an equal sensor (2–5%). The gap is MODIS sensitivity, not random noise.

 







 Reading the app
 
What to keep in mind

 
    
  
  • Before 2012 is an estimate. Where MODIS saw nothing we add what VIIRS usually finds there, so single months in quiet places can be well off.
    • 
  
  • Megafire months are the hardest. They can come out 10–25% low.
    • 
  
  • Outside the tested regions, accuracy varies. The whole world uses the same model, but sparse places (the Arctic, deserts, small islands) are less certain.
    • 
  
  • 2000–2002 are less certain. Only Terra was flying, and the satellite itself was younger than in the test years.
    • 
  
  • Gas flares are left out of every year, so some oil-field areas show less than the raw data.
    • 
  
  • The latest months are provisional. They use near-real-time data, replaced by the final archive a few months later.
    • 
  
  • Small areas are noisier. Error grows as the drawn area shrinks (about 25% for a single 1° block).
    • 
  
  • A detection is not a fire. One large fire can produce hundreds of detections.
    •
