Harmonizing MODIS and VIIRS Active Fire Detections with Shrunk Block Ratios and a Gradient-Boosted Correction

A consistent monthly burning-activity record for any area on Earth, 2001 to present

Om, Ishan, Sparsh, Pratham · Team Murphy, NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026

Challenge

VIIRS on Suomi NPP (375 m)
Inputs
NASA FIRMS MODIS Collection 6.1 (Terra, Aqua) and VIIRS S-NPP Collection 2
Code and data

Abstract

Satellite active-fire records span two sensor families. MODIS, on Terra and Aqua, has observed since November 2000; VIIRS on Suomi NPP, operating since 2012, detects three to six times as many hotspots because of its finer 375 m pixels. Joined without correction, the record shows a step change in 2012 that reflects the new sensor, not the fires: raw counts for the Canadian boreal zone rise 785% from 2011 to 2012. We convert MODIS detections to S-NPP VIIRS-equivalent counts at 1° × 1° block, month and day/night resolution. A ratio of S-NPP to MODIS detections, shrunk from the world to 10° tiles to individual blocks, gives the baseline; a gradient-boosted model with a Poisson loss, using only MODIS-derived inputs, corrects it. The model is fitted on June 2012 to December 2021 after removing years affected by orbit drift, days with sensor outages, and persistent gas flares. In leave-one-year-out tests, monthly totals are within 1.5% worldwide and 2.0–7.3% across eight fire regions, against 2.1–11.3% for our previous rule, and annual totals are within 5% in every region. The harmonized record runs from 2001 to the present and drives an interactive burning-activity calendar.

Keywords: active fire, MODIS, VIIRS, sensor harmonization, gradient boosting, FIRMS, fire regime

For fire managers: reading the numbers

  • Every count is in S-NPP VIIRS-equivalent detections, so 2005 and 2023 can be compared directly.
  • From February 2012 the counts are what S-NPP observed, with gas flares removed. Earlier months are model estimates and are marked ≈ in the calendar.
  • On held-out years, regional monthly totals were within 2–7%. Megafire months and very small areas are the least certain.
  • A detection is a hot pixel, not a fire. Counts measure burning activity, not the number of fires or the area burned. Terms are defined in Appendix C.

1Introduction

NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS) distributes active-fire detections from two families of polar-orbiting sensors. The Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) on Terra and Aqua provides the longest consistent record, beginning in November 2000 with 1 km pixels1. The Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) on Suomi NPP, operating since 2012, detects fires at 375 m and therefore records many small and cool fires that MODIS misses2.

Fire managers and researchers need a single series to judge whether a month is unusual for an area. Neither sensor provides one: MODIS is nearing the end of its mission, and VIIRS does not reach back before 2012. This report describes how we convert the MODIS era into VIIRS-equivalent counts, how we tested the conversion on years the model never saw, and where it remains uncertain.

1.1Why the two sensors disagree

An active-fire product does not count fires; it counts pixels whose thermal signal exceeds a detection threshold. About seven VIIRS 375 m pixels fit inside one MODIS 1 km pixel. A small fire fills a large fraction of a VIIRS pixel and is detected, while the same fire occupies a few percent of a MODIS pixel and often is not (Figure 1). The ratio of VIIRS to MODIS detections therefore depends on fire size, intensity and type, and a single fixed conversion factor cannot capture it.

1.2Effect on the combined record

Figure 2 shows yearly detections for the area currently selected in the calendar. Joined without correction, the arrival of S-NPP in 2012 appears as a sharp rise in fire. MODIS alone, a single instrument observing both years, gives the real change between 2011 and 2012; the harmonized series should follow it.

1.3Objective

The objective is a monthly series of S-NPP-equivalent detections for every 0.25° cell on Earth from 2001 to the present, such that any user-selected area can be compared across the full period. The reference is S-NPP VIIRS because it is the more sensitive sensor and continues into the future; MODIS-era months are converted into its units rather than the reverse.

2Data and preprocessing

2.1Detections and units of analysis

We use FIRMS active-fire detections from MODIS Collection 6.1 (Terra and Aqua)1 and VIIRS 375 m from S-NPP2, restricted to presumed vegetation fires (type 0) at nominal or high confidence; NOAA-20 VIIRS is used only to estimate the noise floor (§7.2). Models are fitted on one unit throughout: a 1° block (about 110 km) in one month, split into the day and night overpasses. Results are distributed to 0.25° cells for display (§6).

2.2Study period

The two sensor families overlap from February 2012. Terra and Aqua overpass times were stable until 2021 and then drifted (Figure 3), so the period after 2021 no longer resembles the 2000–2011 MODIS era. Fitting and testing are therefore restricted to June 2012 – December 2021, and the fitted model is applied backwards to November 2000 – January 2012.

2.3Corrections applied before modelling

Four problems in the paired record were found by returning to the individual detections. Correcting them accounts for roughly half of the improvement over our previous rule.

  1. Orbit drift. Terra's mean overpass moved from 10:33 to 09:26 and Aqua's from 13:36 to 14:45 by 2025. Fire activity changes through the day, so the S-NPP/MODIS ratio shifts with them. Fitting stops at December 2021.
  2. Outage days. Aqua was unavailable from 16 August to 2 September 2020; S-NPP lost 16 days in 2022 and 25 in 2024; Terra 11 days in February 2016. When any sensor is missing, the day is removed for all of them, so each pair compares the same days. In MODIS-era months, totals are scaled by days in month ÷ days retained.
  3. Gas flares. Oil-field flares burn year-round, mostly at night, where S-NPP is far more sensitive than MODIS. They are industrial sources, not wildfire. A 0.02° cell is masked when, over 2012–2021,
    \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{active in} \ge 8 \text{ of the 12 calendar months},\\ &\text{active in} \ge 15 \text{ distinct months, and}\\ &\ge 50\% \text{ of its detections at night.} \end{aligned} \tag{1} \]
    A 0.1° cell is also masked when active in ≥ 30 months, ≥ 10 calendar months and ≥ 75% at night, to capture well fields that move. The mask removes about 1.7% of S-NPP detections; seasonal, daytime crop and savanna burning is unaffected.
  4. S-NPP commissioning. In March 2012 the S-NPP/MODIS ratio was about 20% below its 2013–2019 mean. Fitting begins in June 2012.

3Baseline: shrunk block ratios

The simplest conversion is the ratio of S-NPP to MODIS detections over the training period,

\[ r = \frac{\sum S}{\sum M}, \tag{2} \]

where \(S\) and \(M\) are S-NPP and MODIS detection counts. A single ratio for the world is too coarse, since crown fires in the boreal forest and savanna fires in Africa behave differently. A ratio per 1° block is local but unstable where a block has few MODIS detections. Each level is therefore shrunk toward the level above it:

\[ r_{\text{block}} = \frac{\sum S_{\text{block}} + k\, r_{\text{tile}}}{\sum M_{\text{block}} + k} = w\,\frac{\sum S_{\text{block}}}{\sum M_{\text{block}}} + (1-w)\, r_{\text{tile}}, \tag{3} \]

with weight \(w = \sum M_{\text{block}} \big/ \left(\sum M_{\text{block}} + k\right)\). Equation 3 treats the block as if it had \(k\) additional MODIS detections behaving like its 10° tile. With \(k = 200\), a block with 200 MODIS detections of history receives half weight on its own ratio and one with 2,000 about 91% (Figure 4). Tile ratios are shrunk toward the world ratio in the same way with \(k = 2{,}000\). All ratios are computed separately for day and night.

Ratios are also computed per block × calendar month and per tile × calendar month. They are not multiplied into the baseline; they are supplied to the correction model (§4) as inputs, so that the strength of the seasonal effect is learned.

4Gradient-boosted correction

The block ratio is a long-run average. In a given month it can be biased: a month of intense crown fires yields fewer S-NPP detections per MODIS detection than a month of small smouldering fires in the same block. The baseline is therefore multiplied by a correction learned from that month's MODIS observations:

\[ \hat S = M\, r_{\text{block}}\; e^{f(x)} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln \hat S = \ln\!\left(M\, r_{\text{block}}\right) + f(x), \tag{4} \]

where \(\hat S\) is the estimated S-NPP count, \(M\) the MODIS count in the same block, month and half of the day, and \(f(x)\) a learned function of the inputs \(x\). \(f = 0\) leaves the baseline unchanged; \(f = 0.2\) raises it by about 22%.

4.1Inputs

Because the model must run in 2000–2011, every input is derived from MODIS alone: the block-month's own detections, its neighbours and its context (Table 2).

4.2Gradient-boosted trees

The function \(f\) is an additive ensemble of 400 regression trees fitted by gradient boosting with LightGBM3, each tree correcting the residual of those before it:

\[ f(x) = \eta \sum_{t=1}^{400} T_t(x), \qquad \eta = 0.05. \tag{5} \]

Each tree partitions the inputs by a sequence of binary splits. Its leaves are linear: a leaf returns a linear function of the inputs rather than a constant, so the correction varies smoothly within a leaf (Figure 5).

4.3Loss function

Detections are counts whose variance grows with their mean, so a squared-error loss would be dominated by a few very large fire months. The model minimises the Poisson deviance, equivalently

\[ \ell(S, \hat S) = \hat S - S \ln \hat S, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \ln \hat S} = \hat S - S, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial (\ln \hat S)^2} = \hat S. \tag{6} \]

Each boosting step moves the prediction toward the residual \(S - \hat S\) scaled by \(1/\hat S\), so errors are weighted in relative rather than absolute terms.

4.4Leakage control and settings

The block ratios supplied as inputs are recomputed without the row's own year. Otherwise each row's ratio would already contain its target, the model would learn to over-trust the ratio, and that trust would fail in the MODIS era, where no S-NPP data exist to build it.

LightGBM · objective poisson · 400 rounds · learning rate 0.05 · 31 leaves · ≥ 100 rows per leaf · 80% feature and row subsampling · L2 = 5 · linear leaves

5Months without a MODIS signal

Equation 4 is proportional to \(M\) and returns zero when MODIS detected nothing in a block and half of the day. S-NPP frequently does detect fire there, precisely the small fires MODIS misses. Block-months are therefore assigned to one of three estimators (Table 3).

5.1The Terra-only period

Aqua began observing in July 2002, so from November 2000 to June 2002 MODIS means Terra alone, about three hours before S-NPP's overpass. A second instance of the full model is fitted on 2012–2021 with Aqua removed and applied to those months. Removing Aqua from the overlap years also provides a direct test of this path (Table 4, last column).

6Downscaling and the 2012 join

The calendar and map use 0.25° cells, sixteen per block. Each block estimate is distributed by the location of its MODIS detections:

\[ \hat S_{\text{cell}} = \hat S_{\text{block}} \cdot \frac{M_{\text{cell}}}{M_{\text{block}}}. \tag{7} \]

Where MODIS detected nothing in the block (zero model and climatology), each cell receives its share of the block's S-NPP detections over 2012–2021. Fill values below 0.5 in cells with no MODIS detection are dropped to keep the distributed files small (0.24% of the total). From February 2012 nothing is estimated: the harmonized record is the observed S-NPP count with gas flares removed, so both eras measure the same quantity:

\[ H = \begin{cases} \hat S, & \text{November 2000 – January 2012} \\ S_{\text{observed}} - S_{\text{flares}}, & \text{February 2012 onward.} \end{cases} \tag{8} \]

The join is tested at the seam. If the conversion is correct, the change from 2011 to 2012 should match the change seen by MODIS alone. Worldwide, the harmonized record rises 5.6% from 2011 to 2012 (previous rule: 6.8%). For the Canadian boreal zone it rises 59.8%, against 60.5% for MODIS alone and 785% in the raw sum; 2012 was a larger fire year there (Figure 2).

7Validation

7.1Protocol

Performance is estimated by leave-one-year-out cross-validation over 2012–2021: the model is fitted on nine years and predicts the tenth from MODIS alone, ten times (Figure 6). Every prediction is therefore for a year the model did not see, as in the MODIS era. All rules in Tables 1 and 4 are fitted and scored on the same cleaned data. The score is the weighted absolute percentage error of monthly totals per region (day and night, all blocks inside the region's bounds),

\[ \text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{\text{months}} \lvert \hat S - S \rvert}{\sum_{\text{months}} S}, \tag{9} \]

the share of observed detections that the estimate gets wrong. Unlike a mean of monthly percentage errors, it is not dominated by months with very few detections.

7.2Noise floor

NOAA-20 carries the same VIIRS instrument as S-NPP and crosses about 50 minutes apart. Predicting S-NPP from NOAA-20 with block ratios over 2019–2025 measures the error between two identical sensors, a practical lower bound for any MODIS-based rule.

7.3Results

Monthly error is below 5% worldwide and in six of eight regions, and annual totals are within 5% everywhere (Figure 7, Table 4). Against the previous rule, the method roughly halves the error in US West, Siberia and Northern Australia and reduces it by about 40% in Canada.

7.4Additional checks

  • Forward holdout. Fitted on 2012–2018 and tested on 2019–2021: Canada 6.7%, Amazon 2.7%, Mediterranean 6.9%, US West 6.1%, Siberia 4.2%, world 1.7%, against 15.2%, 2.5%, 6.5%, 12.1%, 8.7% and 2.8% for the previous rule.
  • Drift years. Under the earlier protocol (fit 2012–2020, test 2021–2023) the method still leads in Canada (5.8% vs 9.7%), US West (4.1% vs 12.9%) and worldwide (2.9% vs 3.7%), but its mean monthly error is higher in the Amazon and Mediterranean, because it treats Terra and Aqua separately and is more exposed to their drift.
  • Random seeds. Averaging three seeds changes no score by more than 0.1 points.
  • Flare-mask leakage. The mask is built from all of 2012–2021, including each held-out year. Rebuilding it without the held-out year changes the status of 0.2–0.4% of that year's S-NPP detections, which bounds the effect on the scores at about half a point.

7.5Error analysis

Canada: megafire months. Six months account for about 60% of the error, including the Northwest Territories in July 2014 (−24%) and British Columbia in August 2018 (−11%). In these months the S-NPP/MODIS ratio rises from about 5 to 10–29 per block, as smoke and smouldering ground hide fire from MODIS. Leave-one-year-out removes the only comparable event from each fit, so this figure is likely pessimistic for the final model fitted on all ten years.

Mediterranean: fires MODIS does not register. About 8% of S-NPP detections fall in block-months with no same-time MODIS detection. No MODIS-derived input can describe them, and quiet winter months consist mostly of such detections.

The noise floor shows that these months are predictable to 2–5% with an equivalent sensor. The remaining gap reflects MODIS sensitivity, not random noise.

8Approaches considered

The method was reached through three stages, each evaluated by held-out testing (Table 5). The full sequence, including approaches that were tested and not adopted, is given in Appendix A.

Gradient-boosted trees were also tried in the v1 search, where they gave similar error to the blended rule with more bias. They became the better choice only after the data corrections of §2.3 and the restriction to stable-orbit years, which suggests that the cleaning, more than the model class, removed the main source of error.

9Limitations and intended use

The harmonized record is intended for comparing burning activity across months and years within an area, for example to judge whether a month is unusual against its 2003–2020 baseline. It is not a count of fires or of area burned, and it is not intended for single-cell or single-day analysis.

  • Months before 2012 are estimates. Where MODIS detected nothing, the estimate is the typical S-NPP count for that place and season, so individual months in quiet areas can be substantially off.
  • Megafire months are underestimated by 10–25% in the worst cases (§7.5).
  • Accuracy outside the eight test regions varies. The same global model is applied everywhere, but sparse areas such as the Arctic, deserts and small islands are less certain.
  • 2000–2002 are less certain: only Terra was observing, and the satellite was younger than in the test years.
  • Gas flares are removed from every year, so some oil-field areas show fewer detections than the raw data.
  • Recent months are provisional: they use near-real-time data, replaced by the standard archive a few months later.
  • Error grows as the area shrinks, to about 25% for a single 1° block.

ADevelopment history

This appendix records how the method developed, including approaches that were tested and not adopted. Errors from different stages were measured under different protocols and are comparable only within a stage; Table 1 rescores the three main rules on a common protocol.

A.1Pipeline and reference sensor

S-NPP was chosen as the single VIIRS reference, with NOAA-20 held back for the noise floor. Detections were aggregated to a 0.25° monthly grid. A first calibration computed ratios per cell-month where both sensors detected fire; this was too sparse and the ratios collapsed toward 1.0, so ratios were instead aggregated over regions and strata. The pipeline was checked on synthetic data with a planted ratio of 2.8, which it recovered as 2.66 (within 5%). Synthetic data were used only for this check and never reported as results.

A.2First rule: single ratio per stratum

One ratio per region for each season (fire season May–September, off-season) and day/night, calibrated on 2012–2020 and tested on 2021–2023. Mean monthly error on that holdout was 19.6% in Canada (16.9% with global ratios), 17.9% in the Amazon and 20.6% in the Mediterranean. A separate Terra-only ratio was applied before July 2002.

A.3Counting unit

Counting each 0.05° cell at most once per day, instead of every detection, removes repeat detections of the same fire and shrinks the ratios considerably (Canada's fire-season night ratio from 8.42 to 2.65). It lowered holdout error only in the Amazon, by 0.6 points, and made Canada and the Mediterranean clearly worse, including at the 2012 join. Detection counts were kept.

A.4Rule search (v1)

About 90 variants were scored by leave-one-year-out across eight regions: ratio variants, per-area ratios, power laws, Poisson regression, fire-size (FRP) bins, gradient boosting, blends and confidence filters. The chosen rule averaged a shrunk 1° block ratio with a region-level monthly power law. Against the single ratio it reduced total error from 12.7% to 8.9% and mean absolute bias from 10.4% to 4.2%. Because those rounds included 2021–2023, a nested check chose the block size, shrinkage and blend weight using 2012–2020 only and scored once on the untouched 2021–2023 holdout: mean error over eight regions fell from 14.3% to 11.0%. Not adopted at this stage: global ratios, separate Terra and Aqua predictors, FRP bins, region-specific fire seasons, power laws on small blocks, high-confidence-only counts, and gradient-boosted trees (similar error, more bias). An independent recomputation of the shipped Canada record in SQL reproduced every cell's value to within rounding.

A.5Return to the raw detections (v2)

Rebuilding the paired tables from the individual detections exposed the orbit drift, outage days, gas flares and S-NPP commissioning period described in §2.3. With these removed and fitting restricted to stable-orbit years, the NOAA-20 noise floor showed that months were predictable to within a few percent, so the remaining error lay in the rule, not the data.

A.6Land-cover prior

Ratios by biome from the MODIS land-cover product (MCD12C1) explained 11–43% of the spread of block ratios within regions, but gave no out-of-sample gain over shrunk block ratios, and world-pooled biome ratios were worse. The idea was dropped.

A.7Current method

One worldwide model at 1° block × month × day/night: shrunk block ratios (§3), a Poisson gradient-boosted correction with linear leaves (§4), separate estimators where MODIS saw nothing (§5), and a Terra-only variant for 2000–2002. MODIS burned-area data (MCD64A1) as an additional input for megafire months has not yet been tried.

BWorked example

An illustrative 1° block in the Canadian boreal forest, daytime, in an active July. The controls set the inputs of Eqs. 3 and 4; in the fitted model, \(f(x)\) is produced by the trees rather than set by hand. A positive \(f\) corresponds to months of many small, low-confidence fires, a negative \(f\) to months of a few very intense ones.

CGlossary

Detection (hotspot)
A satellite pixel flagged as containing an active fire at the time of the overpass. One large fire can produce hundreds of detections.
MODIS
Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer on the Terra (from 2000) and Aqua (from 2002) satellites; 1 km fire pixels.
VIIRS, S-NPP
Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite on the Suomi NPP satellite (from 2012); 375 m fire pixels. The reference sensor for this record.
Harmonized count
Detections expressed in S-NPP VIIRS units: observed from February 2012, estimated from MODIS before.
Estimate (≈)
A month before February 2012, converted from MODIS by the method in this report.
Provisional
A recent month based on near-real-time data, which may change slightly when the standard archive is published.
× typical
A month's count divided by the median of the same calendar month over 2003–2020.
Block, cell
A 1° × 1° area (about 110 km) used for modelling; a 0.25° × 0.25° area (about 25 km) used for display.
FRP
Fire radiative power: the heat output of a detection, in megawatts.
WAPE
Weighted absolute percentage error: total absolute error divided by total observed detections (Eq. 9).
